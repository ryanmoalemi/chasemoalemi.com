The work

Auto parts is a data business

My brother Ryan and I run an online auto parts business on Shopify. I handle operations, and most of my attention goes to the catalog, because in this category the catalog is the product.

A customer is not buying "brake pads." They are buying the brake pads that fit their car, down to the engine, the trim and sometimes the brake package it left the factory with. If the data is wrong, the sale turns into a return, a shipping bill and a customer who doesn't come back.

So the work I find most interesting is using AI to make a messy catalog accurate, consistent and easy to trust.

Why auto parts catalogs are hard

  • Year, make and model is not enough

    Fitment in the aftermarket is described with the ACES standard, mapped to a shared vehicle database called the VCdb. A base vehicle is only the start. Engine, submodel, drive type and qualifiers like "with rear disc brakes" often decide whether a part fits. Two cars with the same badge can need different parts.

  • One part, hundreds of vehicles

    A single part can fit a long list of vehicle configurations. Shopify was built around products and variants, not one-to-many fitment, so that data has to live in a fitment app or in metafields. Getting it there without flattening it into vague tags is a real design decision.

  • Part numbers change

    Manufacturers supersede part numbers, replacing an old number with a new one. Customers and shops still search the old number. A catalog that doesn't know both is invisible to some of its best buyers.

  • Interchange is a map, not a guess

    Interchange and cross-reference data tell you which part numbers can replace each other. It is how a buyer holding an OE number finds an aftermarket equivalent. It is also easy to get subtly wrong, and subtle errors are the expensive kind.

  • Every supplier speaks a different dialect

    Product details travel in the PIES standard, but supplier files still arrive with different naming, units, abbreviations and gaps. The same rotor can show up three ways from three sources. Before anything else, it has to become one clean record.

  • Every channel wants its own version

    Your store, Google Shopping and marketplaces like eBay each have their own way of expressing compatibility. The same fitment gets translated more than once, and every translation is a chance to lose detail.

Where AI helps, and where it doesn't

  • Helps: normalizing supplier data

    Turning inconsistent titles, attributes and units into one structure is pattern work at volume. Models are good at it, and a person can review the exceptions instead of typing every row.

  • Helps: catching mismatches

    A title that says 4WD on a part whose fitment lists only 2WD. A description that names the wrong engine. A spec that contradicts the attribute file. AI is useful as a second reader that flags conflicts before a customer finds them.

  • Helps: writing from structured facts

    Clear titles and descriptions built from verified attributes read better and search better. The facts come from the data. The model just puts them in order.

  • Doesn't: deciding fitment

    A language model can sound sure about a fitment that does not exist. Fitment should come from the manufacturer's application data and the standards behind it. AI can map and check it. It should not be the source of truth.

  • Doesn't: fixing bad inputs

    If the source data is wrong, a faster pipeline just spreads the mistake to more listings. The real gains are upstream, in what you let into the catalog.

Notes

Things I've learned

  • Data quality is the edge.

    Anyone can list the same parts from the same suppliers. Not everyone can describe them correctly. In a category where trust is the whole sale, accurate data is the advantage that is hard to copy.

  • A return is a data report.

    Most "wrong part" returns are not the customer's fault. They are telling you where the catalog is vague. Read them that way and they become the cheapest audit you will ever get.

  • Make the machine show its work.

    Every AI change to the catalog should point back to a source: a supplier field, a fitment record, a spec sheet. If it can't, it doesn't ship.

  • Systems beat heroics.

    One great day of cleanup fades. A process that cleans every new file the same way compounds.

  • Precision is the customer experience.

    In auto parts, nobody remembers the font on the product page. They remember whether the part bolted on.

Collecting

Cards as an alternative asset

I collect Pokémon and sports cards, and I think about them the way I would think about any alternative asset. That means caring less about what is exciting this week and more about supply, demand, liquidity and what something actually sells for.

It is a surprisingly good mirror of my day job. In both worlds, identity is everything. "Charizard" is not a card any more than "brake pads" is a part. Set, card number, language, print run, parallel and grade all change what you are holding, the same way engine and submodel change what fits.

How I think about the market

  • A pop report is graded supply, not total supply

    PSA, BGS, CGC and SGC each publish population reports showing how many copies they have graded at each grade. That is not the print run. It leaves out raw copies, sealed product and cards graded elsewhere. Always look for the denominator: three gem copies out of thousands submitted means something very different from three copies total.

  • Scarcity without demand is just rare

    A low population number does nothing on its own. Price comes from scarcity meeting buyers who want that exact card. The interesting cards are the ones where both are true.

  • Comps are sales, not asking prices

    Price discovery in cards happens in sold results from auctions and marketplaces. Asking prices tell you what someone hopes for. Recent sales of the same card, same grade, same grader tell you what the market will actually pay.

  • Liquidity is part of the price

    Some cards trade every day. Others can sit for months waiting for the right buyer. A card that is hard to sell is worth less than its last comp suggests, and the spread between buyers and sellers widens fast in a soft market.

  • Pokémon and sports cards are different markets

    Pokémon demand is driven by characters, artwork and nostalgia, and it is global, with a strong Japanese market alongside English releases. Sports card demand follows players. Rookie seasons, injuries, playoff runs and retirements move prices, so it reacts to news in a way Pokémon mostly doesn't.

  • Sealed product is its own position

    Sealed boxes trade on the supply left unopened. Every box that gets opened makes the remaining ones scarcer and adds singles to the market. Holding sealed is a bet on time and on how fast everyone else opens theirs.

  • Costs are real

    Grading fees, seller fees, shipping and insurance come out of every trade. Over short holding periods they can eat most of the gain. Patience is not a personality trait here. It is math.

  • Provenance and condition carry the value

    A slab from a trusted grader is a form of verification that makes a card easier to sell to a stranger. That is why grading exists, and why the grader on the label matters as much as the number.
